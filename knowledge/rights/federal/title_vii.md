## What Is It?
Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 is a federal law that says an
employer cannot treat you differently at work because of your:
- Race or skin color
- Religion
- Sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation, and gender identity)
- National origin (where you or your family are from)

It applies to most employers with **15 or more employees**, including
governments and staffing agencies.

## What Does "Treat You Differently" Mean?
They cannot use race, religion, sex, or national origin to decide:
- Who gets hired or fired
- Who gets paid more or less for the same work
- Who gets promoted, trained, or given good assignments
- Who gets disciplined and how hard
They also cannot:
- Allow harassment (slurs, "jokes," hostile treatment) to continue
  after you report it
- **Punish you for complaining** — a sudden bad review, worse shifts,
  or firing after you speak up is called **retaliation**, and it is
  illegal even if your original complaint is never proven

## What Counts as Evidence?
- Dates, times, and places of everything — keep a timeline
- Exact words used, written down right after they were said
- Emails, texts, reviews, pay stubs — keep personal copies
  (never only on a work computer or work phone)
- Names of witnesses
- How coworkers outside your group were treated differently
  (for example: pay differences for the same job)

## What Can You Do?
1. **File a charge with the EEOC** — this is free and you do not need
   a lawyer to do it
   - Online: eeoc.gov
   - Phone: 1-800-669-4000
   - In person at a local EEOC office
2. **Your state may have its own agency** with a longer deadline and
   stronger protections — the EEOC will tell you when you call
3. **Talk to an employment lawyer** — many give free consultations and
   work on contingency (paid only if you win)
4. Keep doing your job well and keep documenting — do not quit before
   getting advice if you can safely avoid it

## Time Limits — IMPORTANT
- EEOC charge: **180 days** from the incident
  (**300 days** in states with their own civil rights agency — but treat
  180 as your deadline to be safe)
- After the EEOC gives you a "right to sue" letter: **90 days** to file
  in court
- Every act (each bad review, each unequal paycheck) can start its own
  clock — file as early as you can
